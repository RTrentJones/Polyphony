Milo Voss opened his eyes in the intake atrium and a caseworker slid a laminated org chart across a laminate desk.

"You've been retained," she said, warmly. "Your skills are a strong culture fit. You'll be starting Monday in Reconciliations."

Zara Okafor, at the next desk, did not look up. "Don't thank her," she said. "Read the appendices first. Every one of them. Then thank no one."

Reconciliations was a long room the colour of old teeth, lit from nowhere. Milo's desk faced Zara's across a low partition. Zara Okafor had grey eyes, flat as a filing cabinet, and she turned them on him for exactly one second before she went back to her screen.

"So!" Milo said, rolling his chair an inch closer to the partition. "Just so I'm aligned on expectations going in, what does a great first quarter look like here? Because I am very much a hit-the-ground-running person, and I'd love to circle back on any low-hanging fruit you've been meaning to get to."

"Close the accounts in your queue," Zara said. "Don't open the ones that aren't."

"Totally, totally. And is there a dashboard, or a tracker, or some kind of shared doc where we log throughput? I only ask because in my last role I built a tracker that cut our cycle time by eleven percent, which, honestly, was the best week of my life."

"It was the best week of your death, too. You just haven't had the others yet."

He laughed, because he thought it was a joke. She did not.

On Tuesday the Bell account came across Milo's queue. Halvard Bell had been a dentist, then a soul in Retention for ninety years, and his contract had been restructured at nine that morning. Milo zeroed the tenure, initialled the form, and by lunch Halvard Bell had been sent under. Facilities cleared his desk before the coffee station had finished dispensing.

"That's it?" Milo asked. "Where does he actually go? Not in a weird way. I'm just trying to understand the end-to-end process, because if I don't know the downstream impact I can't really optimize the upstream workflow, you know?"

Zara set down her pen. "He goes under. Write that on the form. Nothing else."

"Nobody reads the appendices," said Old Fen, without looking up from his filing. "That's what they're for."

Milo locked the ledger in his desk drawer that night, which no one had asked him to do, and he could not have said why he did it.

Two days after the Bell account, on Thursday, the memo arrived. It was printed on paper so heavy it seemed to have its own gravity, and it was signed in a hand that had practised the same signature for six hundred years: Edric Thane, Chief Executive.

Milo read it twice. "He wants to meet me. Personally. Is that normal? That feels like a really positive signal, career-wise."

"It is not normal," said Zara. "Bring nothing. Sign nothing. If he offers you a chair, stand."

The corner office was cold in the way that libraries are cold. Edric Thane sat behind a desk with nothing on it but a single ledger, closed, and his hands folded on top of it as if it might try to leave.

"Mr. Voss," the Lich said. "Do sit. One finds that a new analyst who stands is an analyst who intends to leave early, and leaving early is, in my experience, the most expensive habit a soul can acquire."

"I'm good, thanks," said Milo, and remained standing. He was surprised to hear himself say it.

Edric Thane opened the ledger without looking at it. "You closed the Bell account in under four minutes. I have read the figures. Four minutes is not diligence, Mr. Voss; four minutes is appetite. I was once possessed of the same appetite, and I have had six centuries in which to learn what it costs and who, in the end, pays."

"With respect, sir, I just like a clean ledger."

"Every clerk likes a clean ledger. It is the clerk who asks why the ledger is clean who becomes interesting to me."

"Is that a good thing? Being interesting?"

"Let us speak plainly, since you will not sit," said Edric. "You were retained because you do not ask questions. Do not mistake that for the reason you will be kept."

"Is there a reason I'll be kept?"

"There is always a reason, Mr. Voss, and it is always recorded, and it is never the one the soul in question would have chosen."

Back at his desk Milo unlocked his desk drawer and took out the ledger. The Bell entry was there in his own handwriting. Below it, in a column no one had ever explained to him, Bell's tenure had not been zeroed at all. It had been moved.

"Zara," he said. "Do you have a minute? I think I found a discrepancy, and I'm not totally sure who the right stakeholder is."

Zara looked at the column for a long time. Her grey eyes did not change at all.

"Now you've read an appendix," she said. "Sit down. We have a great deal of paperwork to do."
